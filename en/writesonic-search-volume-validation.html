Можно ли оценивать поисковый спрос через Writesonic: проверка на живых данных

Ask a writing assistant to sort fifty keywords into "high", "medium" and "low" demand and it will do it in seconds, with no hesitation and no footnotes. The problem is that a large language model has no live connection to search statistics unless it is explicitly given one. If those labels go straight into a content plan, you may spend months writing for demand that only existed in a model's guess. This article gives you a protocol to measure the gap yourself: a discrepancy table, a scoring script and a safe pre-selection process that keeps the model useful without letting it invent numbers.

Level: intermediate · Reading time: ~50 minutes · Updated: 2 October 2026

What this article is and is not. It is a test protocol with tools and decision rules. It does not contain measured results from our own run: we do not publish a discrepancy figure here because we have not verified one we are willing to stand behind. Every number in the worked example below is synthetic and labelled as such; it exists only so you can check that the script works before you load your real data. Product behaviour of Writesonic can change between plans and releases — check what your account actually shows before relying on any statement about features.

1. The real problem: confident labels without a source

Search demand is an empirical quantity. Somebody — a search engine, or a vendor who buys or models clickstream data — counted or estimated how many times a phrase was searched in a given region over a given period. Any number that claims to represent demand should be traceable to such a count.

A language model producing text in a chat or a document editor works differently. It predicts plausible words. When asked "what is the search volume for crm for dentists?", the plausible continuation is a number or a label, so that is what it produces. Unless the product has connected it to a data source and passes real figures into the context, the answer is a reconstruction from patterns in training text: how often similar phrases were discussed, how "commercial" they sound, how long they are. That is a form of hallucination — not because the number is necessarily wrong, but because nothing in the process guarantees that it is right.

Three properties make this dangerous for content planning specifically:

  • The output looks like data. A neat table with "High / Medium / Low" columns reads like a report, not like a guess.
  • Errors are not random noise. Models tend to overrate phrases that sound important or are widely written about, and underrate long, specific, local or newly emerging queries — exactly the queries small sites can win.
  • Feedback is slow. You find out an article targeted a dead query three to six months after publishing, when the traffic does not come. By then the plan has been executed.

Writesonic is a useful example because it is a popular writing platform that offers both free-form chat and SEO-oriented features. Depending on plan and feature, a number on screen may come from an integrated data source or may be produced by the model. The practical question is therefore not "is Writesonic good or bad" but: for the specific screen and workflow you use, are the demand levels backed by statistics, and how far off are they if they are not? That is measurable in an afternoon.

2. A concrete case: the content plan that looked fine

Here is the scenario this protocol is designed for. It is a composite description of a typical workflow, not a report about a specific client.

A small B2B team selling appointment software wants a quarterly blog plan. A marketer opens a chat-style assistant and asks: "Give me 40 keywords around online booking for clinics, with search volume level and difficulty." The assistant returns a table. Twelve rows are marked "High", including several broad phrases; fifteen are "Medium"; the rest "Low". The team picks the "High + Low difficulty" quadrant, which looks like an obvious win, and schedules eight articles.

Nobody asked where "High" came from. Nobody wrote down which region, which language, which search engine, or which month the label referred to. The plan has no field that would let anyone later separate "a statistics tool said 2,400/month in Germany" from "the model thought this sounded popular".

The fix is not to stop using the assistant. It is to add a measurement step and a provenance field, which is what the rest of this article builds.

3. Turning "can we trust it?" into a testable question

"Is it accurate?" is too vague to test. Split it into three questions, each with its own metric:

QuestionWhy it matters for planningMetric
Does it put keywords in the right bucket (low / medium / high)?Most plans use buckets, not exact numbers.Bucket agreement rate; confusion matrix.
Does it get the order right?If you only pick the top 20 of 100 ideas, order matters more than absolute values.Spearman rank correlation between estimate and reference.
How large are the numeric errors when it gives numbers?Traffic forecasts and prioritisation scores multiply these numbers.Median absolute log10 error; share of estimates within ×2 and ×10 of reference.

Write down your acceptance thresholds before you see any results. If you set them afterwards, you will unconsciously set them to whatever makes the tool look acceptable. A reasonable starting point for a pre-selection filter (adjust to your risk tolerance):

# thresholds.yaml — decide BEFORE running the comparison
bucket_agreement_min: 0.70     # at least 70% of keywords in the same bucket
spearman_min: 0.60             # ordering broadly right
within_x2_min: 0.50            # half of numeric estimates within a factor of 2
gross_miss_max: 0.10           # at most 10% of estimates off by more than x10
false_high_max: 0.10           # at most 10% of reference-low keywords labelled "high"

The last threshold deserves attention. A keyword with real demand labelled "low" costs you an opportunity. A keyword with no real demand labelled "high" costs you an article. For content planning the second error is usually more expensive, so it gets its own limit.

4. Setup: what you need before you start

  • A Writesonic account with access to the feature you actually use for keyword ideas (chat, SEO tools, or both). Record the plan name and the date of the test; behaviour can differ between them.
  • A reference source with real statistics for your target market. Options include Google Keyword Planner (Google Ads account required; exact figures may be shown as ranges for accounts without active spend), Yandex Wordstat for Russian-language demand in Yandex, or a commercial SEO database such as Ahrefs, Semrush or similar. Each of these is itself an estimate with its own method — see Limitations — but it is grounded in counted data, which is the point.
  • Python 3.9+ for the scoring script. No third-party packages are required.
  • A spreadsheet to inspect the table by eye. The script is the source of truth; the spreadsheet is for reading.

Fix these parameters and write them at the top of your test log, because every reference number depends on them:

# run.yaml
run_id: 2026-10-ws-volume-01
date: 2026-10-02
writesonic_feature: "chat"        # or the exact name of the SEO screen you used
writesonic_plan: "<your plan>"
reference_tool: "Google Keyword Planner"
search_engine: google
country: DE
language: de
period: "last 12 months, monthly average"
match_type: "exact phrase as entered"

5. Step 1. Build a keyword sample that can actually fail

A common mistake is to test with keywords that any tool will get right: "crm", "booking software". Big head terms are easy; the risk lives in the tail. Build a sample of 60–100 keywords that deliberately covers several strata:

StratumShareExample shape (illustrative)What it tests
Head terms~15%"appointment software"Baseline; should be easy.
Mid-tail commercial~25%"online booking for physiotherapists"Typical blog targets.
Long-tail questions~25%"how to reduce no-shows at a dental clinic"Where models tend to guess.
Local / language-specific~15%a phrase in your target language with a city nameRegional bias.
"Sounds important" traps~10%industry jargon that appears in articles more than in searchesFalse "high".
Known zero / near-zero~10%plausible but rarely searched phrasingsWhether the tool ever says "very low".

Two rules make the sample honest:

  1. Do not choose keywords by looking at reference volumes first. Draft the list from customer language, sales call notes or your own ideas, then freeze it.
  2. Freeze the list in a file with a hash, so you can prove later that it was not edited after seeing results.
# keywords.csv — one column, UTF-8, no header edits after freezing
keyword
appointment software
online booking for physiotherapists
how to reduce no-shows at a dental clinic
...

# freeze it
sha256sum keywords.csv > keywords.csv.sha256

6. Step 2. Collect Writesonic estimates in a reproducible way

If you use a feature that displays numbers from an integrated data source, record that the column came from that screen and note any source label shown. If you use chat, use a fixed prompt and a fresh conversation, so earlier messages do not steer the answer. The prompt below asks for both a bucket and a number, and gives the model an explicit way to say it does not know — a well-behaved system should use it.

You will receive a list of search queries.
Market: Google, Germany, German language, monthly average over the last 12 months.

For each query return one CSV row with these columns:
keyword,ws_bucket,ws_volume,ws_source
- ws_bucket: one of very_low, low, medium, high
  (very_low <10, low 10-99, medium 100-999, high >=1000 searches per month)
- ws_volume: your numeric estimate of monthly searches, or "unknown"
- ws_source: where the number comes from; write "model_estimate" if it is
  not taken from a search statistics database

Return only CSV, no commentary. Keep the keyword text exactly as given.

Queries:
<paste keywords.csv contents>

Practical details that affect reproducibility:

  • Batch size. Send 20–25 keywords per message. Long lists increase the chance of skipped or rewritten rows.
  • Repeat the run. Run the same prompt three times in three fresh chats. If the model's estimates are not based on data, they will often vary between runs; that variance is itself evidence. Save each run as ws_run1.csv, ws_run2.csv, ws_run3.csv.
  • Save raw output. Paste the raw answer into a text file before cleaning it. If you later suspect a parsing error, you need the original.
  • Record the ws_source column honestly. If the assistant claims a database source, check whether the product actually exposes one on your plan. A claimed source without a visible integration is still a model estimate.

7. Step 3. Pull reference data from a statistics tool

Load the same frozen list into your reference tool with the market settings from run.yaml. Export to CSV and reduce it to two columns:

# reference.csv
keyword,ref_volume
appointment software,<number from the tool>
online booking for physiotherapists,<number from the tool>
...

Things that silently break the comparison at this step:

  • Keyword normalisation. Tools may lowercase, strip punctuation or merge close variants. Join on a normalised key (lowercase, collapsed spaces), and log any keyword the tool merged or rewrote.
  • Ranges instead of numbers. If the tool returns "100–1K", store the geometric midpoint (≈316) for numeric metrics, and the range's bucket for bucket metrics. Mark these rows; they add uncertainty.
  • Missing rows. "No data" is not zero in every tool. Record it as empty, not as 0, and decide in advance whether such rows count as "very_low" or are excluded. The script below excludes them from numeric metrics and reports the count.
  • Different period. A 12-month average and last month's figure can differ several-fold for seasonal queries.

8. Step 4. The discrepancy table

This is the main deliverable. One row per keyword, with the estimate, the reference, and the derived error fields. The script in the next step produces it as discrepancies.csv; this is the column layout:

keywordstratumws_bucketws_volumews_spreadref_volumeref_bucketbucket_matchratioerror_class
Fill in from your own run. We do not publish values here because we have not run a verified comparison for this article.

Column meanings:

  • ws_spread — max/min ratio of the numeric estimate across the three Writesonic runs. A spread above 3 means the tool is not reproducing its own answer.
  • ratio — ws_volume / ref_volume. 1.0 is perfect; 10 is a tenfold overestimate; 0.1 a tenfold underestimate.
  • error_class — one of ok (within ×2), off (×2–×10), gross (beyond ×10), false_high (reference low or very low, estimate high), no_ref, no_estimate.

9. Step 5. Score the discrepancies with a script

Save as score_volume.py. It reads the frozen keyword list (optionally with a stratum column), up to three Writesonic runs and the reference export, writes discrepancies.csv, and prints summary metrics against your thresholds. Standard library only.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Compare LLM keyword-volume estimates with reference statistics."""
import csv, math, re, sys, statistics
from pathlib import Path

BUCKETS = ["very_low", "low", "medium", "high"]
THRESH = {  # keep in sync with thresholds.yaml
    "bucket_agreement_min": 0.70, "spearman_min": 0.60,
    "within_x2_min": 0.50, "gross_miss_max": 0.10, "false_high_max": 0.10,
}

def norm(k): return re.sub(r"\s+", " ", k.strip().lower())

def to_num(v):
    v = (v or "").strip().replace(",", "").replace(" ", "")
    if not v or v.lower() in {"unknown", "n/a", "-"}: return None
    try: return float(v)
    except ValueError: return None

def bucket(n):
    if n is None: return None
    if n < 10: return "very_low"
    if n < 100: return "low"
    if n < 1000: return "medium"
    return "high"

def read(path, key="keyword"):
    with open(path, newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        return {norm(r[key]): r for r in csv.DictReader(f)}

def ranks(xs):
    order = sorted(range(len(xs)), key=lambda i: xs[i])
    r = [0.0] * len(xs); i = 0
    while i < len(order):
        j = i
        while j + 1 < len(order) and xs[order[j + 1]] == xs[order[i]]: j += 1
        for k in range(i, j + 1): r[order[k]] = (i + j) / 2 + 1
        i = j + 1
    return r

def spearman(a, b):
    if len(a) < 3: return None
    ra, rb = ranks(a), ranks(b)
    ma, mb = statistics.mean(ra), statistics.mean(rb)
    num = sum((x - ma) * (y - mb) for x, y in zip(ra, rb))
    den = math.sqrt(sum((x - ma) ** 2 for x in ra) * sum((y - mb) ** 2 for y in rb))
    return num / den if den else None

def main(kw_path, ref_path, *ws_paths):
    kws = read(kw_path); ref = read(ref_path)
    runs = [read(p) for p in ws_paths]
    rows, missing = [], []
    for k, kr in kws.items():
        ests = [to_num(r[k].get("ws_volume")) if k in r else None for r in runs]
        if all(k not in r for r in runs): missing.append(k)
        nums = [e for e in ests if e is not None]
        ws_vol = statistics.median(nums) if nums else None
        spread = (max(nums) / min(nums)) if len(nums) > 1 and min(nums) > 0 else None
        ws_b = next((r[k].get("ws_bucket", "").strip() for r in runs if k in r), "") or bucket(ws_vol)
        ref_vol = to_num(ref[k]["ref_volume"]) if k in ref else None
        ref_b = bucket(ref_vol)
        ratio = (ws_vol / ref_vol) if ws_vol is not None and ref_vol else None
        if ref_vol is None: cls = "no_ref"
        elif ws_vol is None and not ws_b: cls = "no_estimate"
        elif ws_b == "high" and ref_b in ("low", "very_low"): cls = "false_high"
        elif ratio is None: cls = "bucket_only"
        else:
            e = abs(math.log10(ratio)) if ratio > 0 else float("inf")
            cls = "ok" if e <= math.log10(2) else ("off" if e <= 1 else "gross")
        rows.append(dict(keyword=kr["keyword"], stratum=kr.get("stratum", ""),
            ws_bucket=ws_b, ws_volume=ws_vol, ws_spread=spread,
            ref_volume=ref_vol, ref_bucket=ref_b,
            bucket_match=(ws_b == ref_b) if ref_b and ws_b else None,
            ratio=round(ratio, 3) if ratio else None, error_class=cls))

    with open("discrepancies.csv", "w", newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        w = csv.DictWriter(f, fieldnames=list(rows[0].keys())); w.writeheader(); w.writerows(rows)

    scored = [r for r in rows if r["bucket_match"] is not None]
    numeric = [r for r in rows if r["ratio"]]
    low_ref = [r for r in rows if r["ref_bucket"] in ("low", "very_low")]
    m = {
        "keywords": len(rows), "missing_from_ws": len(missing),
        "no_ref": sum(r["error_class"] == "no_ref" for r in rows),
        "bucket_agreement": sum(r["bucket_match"] for r in scored) / len(scored) if scored else None,
        "spearman": spearman([r["ws_volume"] for r in numeric], [r["ref_volume"] for r in numeric]),
        "within_x2": sum(r["error_class"] == "ok" for r in numeric) / len(numeric) if numeric else None,
        "gross_miss": sum(abs(math.log10(r["ratio"])) > 1 for r in numeric) / len(numeric) if numeric else None,
        "false_high": sum(r["error_class"] == "false_high" for r in low_ref) / len(low_ref) if low_ref else None,
        "median_abs_log10": statistics.median(abs(math.log10(r["ratio"])) for r in numeric) if numeric else None,
        "unstable_runs(spread>3)": sum(1 for r in rows if r["ws_spread"] and r["ws_spread"] > 3),
    }
    for k, v in m.items(): print(f"{k:24} {v if not isinstance(v, float) else round(v, 3)}")

    checks = [
        ("bucket_agreement", ">=", THRESH["bucket_agreement_min"]),
        ("spearman", ">=", THRESH["spearman_min"]),
        ("within_x2", ">=", THRESH["within_x2_min"]),
        ("gross_miss", "<=", THRESH["gross_miss_max"]),
        ("false_high", "<=", THRESH["false_high_max"]),
    ]
    print("\nverdict:")
    ok_all = True
    for name, op, t in checks:
        v = m[name]
        ok = v is not None and (v >= t if op == ">=" else v <= t)
        ok_all &= ok
        print(f"  {'PASS' if ok else 'FAIL'} {name} {op} {t} (got {v})")
    print("  usable as pre-filter" if ok_all else "  NOT usable as a demand source")

    # per-stratum bucket agreement
    strata = sorted({r["stratum"] for r in scored if r["stratum"]})
    for s in strata:
        sub = [r for r in scored if r["stratum"] == s]
        print(f"  stratum {s:20} agreement {sum(r['bucket_match'] for r in sub)/len(sub):.2f} (n={len(sub)})")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    if len(sys.argv) < 4: sys.exit("usage: score_volume.py keywords.csv reference.csv ws_run1.csv [ws_run2.csv ws_run3.csv]")
    main(*sys.argv[1:])

Run it:

sha256sum -c keywords.csv.sha256
python3 score_volume.py keywords.csv reference.csv ws_run1.csv ws_run2.csv ws_run3.csv

The script reports PASS/FAIL per threshold and a per-stratum breakdown. The breakdown is often more informative than the overall verdict: a tool can look acceptable overall because head terms pull the average up while long-tail agreement is poor.

10. Step 6. Verify the run before you read the results

Before trusting any metric, check that the pipeline itself did not distort the data. Each of these takes a minute:

  1. Hash check passes. sha256sum -c keywords.csv.sha256 must print OK. If it does not, the sample changed after freezing.
  2. Row counts match. keywords in the output equals the number of lines in keywords.csv minus the header. missing_from_ws should be 0; if not, the model dropped or rewrote keywords — find them and rerun those rows.
  3. Synthetic sanity test. Create a copy of reference.csv and use it as a fake Writesonic run (rename the column to ws_volume). The script must report bucket agreement 1.0, Spearman 1.0 and within_x2 1.0. If it does not, the join or parsing is broken.
  4. Inverted test. Reverse the order of reference volumes in a fake run. Spearman should come out strongly negative. This confirms the correlation code reacts to order.
  5. Spot-check five rows by hand in the reference tool UI, including at least one gross and one false_high. Exports occasionally shift columns.
  6. Log no_ref count. If more than ~15% of keywords have no reference data, the numeric metrics rest on a biased subset (usually the tail is missing). Say so in your conclusion.
# synthetic sanity test
python3 - <<'EOF'
import csv
rows = list(csv.DictReader(open("reference.csv", encoding="utf-8")))
with open("ws_identity.csv", "w", newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    w = csv.writer(f); w.writerow(["keyword", "ws_bucket", "ws_volume", "ws_source"])
    for r in rows: w.writerow([r["keyword"], "", r["ref_volume"], "identity_test"])
EOF
python3 score_volume.py keywords.csv reference.csv ws_identity.csv

11. Interpreting the outcome

The result will fall into one of three patterns. Decide your response now, not after you see which one you got.

PatternWhat it looks likeWhat to do
A. Data-backedAll thresholds pass; runs are stable (low spread); the tool shows a data source for its numbers.You may use the screen as a first-pass filter for this market. Still re-check final picks in your reference tool and repeat this test quarterly.
B. Order roughly right, numbers unreliableSpearman passes, but within_x2 or gross_miss fails; or long-tail strata fail while head terms pass.Use estimates only to rank brainstormed ideas, never as volume. Every keyword entering the plan needs a reference number.
C. Not a demand signalSpearman low, false_high above limit, large run-to-run spread.Remove demand labels from the workflow entirely. Use the assistant for idea generation and clustering only.

Be careful with what the result supports. A pass on 80 German keywords in one niche says nothing about Russian queries in Yandex or about a different niche. A failure says the tool was not reliable for this market and this feature on this date. Write the scope into the conclusion in exactly those terms.

12. A safe keyword pre-selection process

Whatever your test shows, the following process keeps LLM convenience while making invented demand impossible to slip into the plan. The core idea is a provenance field: every demand number carries the name of the source that produced it, and nothing labelled model_estimate can pass the gate.

  1. Generate. Use Writesonic (or any assistant) to expand seed topics into candidate phrasings, questions and variants. Ask for language real customers would use. Do not ask for volume at this stage — it only anchors you.
  2. Cluster. Have the assistant group candidates by search intent. This is a task language models are generally good at, and errors here are easy to spot by reading.
  3. Measure. Push every candidate through your reference tool for the target market. Write ref_volume, ref_source and ref_date.
  4. Gate. A keyword enters the content plan only if ref_source is a statistics tool and ref_date is within your freshness window (for example, 90 days). Keywords with no data go to a separate "hypothesis" list, not to the plan.
  5. Prioritise. Combine reference volume with business fit and your realistic ability to rank. Model output can help draft the rationale, but the numbers in the score come from the reference tool.
  6. Close the loop. After publishing, compare actual impressions from Search Console (or Yandex Webmaster) with the planning number. Large gaps feed back into your reference-tool trust as well.

A minimal plan schema that enforces this:

# content_plan.csv
keyword,cluster,intent,ref_volume,ref_source,ref_market,ref_date,status
online booking for physiotherapists,booking-niche,commercial,<n>,keyword_planner,DE/de,2026-10-02,planned
how to reduce no-shows at a dental clinic,no-shows,informational,,none,DE/de,,hypothesis

And a gate you can run before every planning meeting:

#!/usr/bin/env python3
# gate_plan.py — fail if any planned keyword lacks real, fresh statistics
import csv, sys, datetime as dt
ALLOWED = {"keyword_planner", "wordstat", "ahrefs", "semrush"}  # edit to your tools
MAX_AGE = 90
bad = []
for r in csv.DictReader(open(sys.argv[1], encoding="utf-8")):
    if r["status"] != "planned": continue
    src = r["ref_source"].strip().lower()
    try: age = (dt.date.today() - dt.date.fromisoformat(r["ref_date"])).days
    except ValueError: age = None
    if src not in ALLOWED or not r["ref_volume"] or age is None or age > MAX_AGE:
        bad.append((r["keyword"], src or "-", r["ref_date"] or "-"))
for b in bad: print("BLOCKED:", *b, sep="\t")
sys.exit(1 if bad else 0)

If you run planning through an agent, put the gate in its tool chain as a hard check rather than an instruction in the prompt. Instructions get ignored under pressure from a long context; an exit code does not.

13. Failure cases to expect in this kind of test

These are failure modes of the method, described so you can recognise them. They are not findings from a specific run.

  • Keyword rewriting. The model "fixes" grammar or translates a phrase, so the row no longer joins. Symptom: missing_from_ws > 0. Fix: smaller batches, explicit instruction to copy text exactly, normalised join.
  • Uniform answers. Most rows get "medium" and round numbers like 500 or 1,000. Bucket agreement may look moderate because many real keywords are medium, while Spearman is near zero. Always read both metrics.
  • Self-reported sources. The model fills ws_source with a database name. Unless the product visibly integrates that data on your plan, treat it as a model estimate.
  • Reference tool blind spots. The reference shows "no data" for most long-tail rows, so the comparison only covers head terms and the tool looks better than it is. Report no_ref and per-stratum numbers.
  • Market mismatch. The prompt said Germany; the reference export was set to "all locations". Every ratio is then biased upward or downward together. The spot-check in step 6 catches this.
  • Seasonality. A query searched mostly in one season compared on "last month" data. Use 12-month averages unless seasonality is the subject of the test.
  • Peeking. Someone removes "unfair" keywords after seeing results. The hash check exists to prevent exactly this.

14. Limitations

  • The reference is also an estimate. Keyword Planner rounds and groups variants; Wordstat counts differently from Google; commercial databases model volumes from their own data. You are measuring agreement with a grounded source, not with ground truth. Where possible, cross-check a subset with impressions from your own Search Console property.
  • Sample size. With 60–100 keywords, a difference of a few percentage points in bucket agreement is within noise. Treat thresholds as coarse gates, not precise scores.
  • Product drift. Writesonic's features, models and data integrations can change. A result is valid for the date, plan and screen in run.yaml.
  • Bucket boundaries are arbitrary. A keyword at 95 vs 105 searches flips buckets with no practical difference. The numeric metrics compensate for this; do not rely on bucket agreement alone.
  • Volume is not traffic. Even perfect volume data says nothing about click-through, SERP features or your ability to rank. This protocol only addresses whether demand numbers are real.
  • No results published here. We describe the method and tooling; we have not published a measured verdict on Writesonic in this article, and you should not cite it as one.

  • ☐ Conclusion states scope (market, feature, date) and the pattern A/B/C.
  • ☐ Content plan has ref_source and ref_date; gate_plan.py runs before planning.

The short answer to the question in the title: you can use Writesonic to find and organise keyword ideas, but whether you can use it to estimate demand is something you should measure for your own market rather than assume — and until you have measured it, every demand label from a model belongs in the "hypothesis" column, not in the plan.
