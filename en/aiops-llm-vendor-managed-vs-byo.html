Who Owns the LLM in AIOps: Vendor-Managed, BYO, and Open Source Compared

Advanced · 20-minute read · Updated 4 November 2026

Choosing an LLM for AIOps is also a decision about ownership. A vendor-managed model minimizes platform work, a bring-your-own model (BYO) preserves model choice, and an open-source model can maximize deployment control. None is automatically the safest or cheapest. The right answer depends on what data may leave your environment, how predictable demand is, and which operational responsibilities your team can actually carry.

The decision is larger than model quality

In an AIOps system, an LLM may summarize incidents, explain alert clusters, propose investigation steps, draft change plans, or translate telemetry into natural language. These tasks are useful, but the model is only one component. The ownership model also determines where prompts are processed, who operates the inference service, which controls are available during an outage, and how much of the total cost is fixed infrastructure rather than per-request usage.

Treat the choice as a boundary-setting exercise. “Vendor-managed” means the AIOps product or an associated provider operates the model endpoint. “BYO” means your organization selects and supplies the model endpoint or provider under the product’s integration contract. “Self-hosted” means your organization operates the inference runtime and the required model artifacts. Open-source model weights may be used in either a managed or self-hosted arrangement, so open source and self-hosted are related but not identical categories.

Working rule: choose the ownership model that keeps the hardest-to-change requirement inside your control boundary. If data residency is non-negotiable, optimize around residency first. If the team cannot operate GPUs, do not treat self-hosting as a free control option.

Three ownership models

1. Vendor-managed

With a vendor-managed model, the AIOps provider owns or operates the inference path. Your system sends an approved request and receives a response through the product’s integration. The provider usually handles model serving, capacity, upgrades, and much of the availability work.

This is often the shortest path to a working incident assistant. It can also reduce the number of components your team must patch and observe. The trade-off is that the provider controls more of the model lifecycle. You may have fewer choices over model version, inference parameters, routing, retention behavior, and fallback behavior.

Vendor-managed is a fit when the product’s data-processing terms, region, retention controls, and operational guarantees satisfy your requirements. It is a poor fit when the vendor cannot provide the controls required for regulated or highly sensitive telemetry.

2. BYO model or endpoint

BYO separates the AIOps product from the model provider. The AIOps layer sends requests to an endpoint selected by your organization, subject to the product’s supported API, authentication, and latency requirements. The endpoint may be a hosted commercial model, a private service, or a gateway that routes to several models.

BYO gives you a useful middle position. You can change model providers without replacing the AIOps product, and you can apply your own gateway policies, redaction, logging, rate limits, and routing. However, “BYO” does not by itself make data private. A hosted endpoint may still process data outside your preferred boundary, and your team may still depend on another provider for availability and retention controls.

BYO is strongest when model choice is strategically important but running inference is not. It is also useful when one model handles summarization well while another handles structured extraction or tool selection more reliably.

3. Self-hosted open-source model

A self-hosted deployment runs inference in infrastructure controlled by your organization or an explicitly trusted infrastructure operator. The model may use publicly available weights, commercially licensed weights, or an internally approved artifact. The important distinction is operational: your team owns the serving path, capacity planning, upgrades, observability, and incident response.

Self-hosting can reduce dependence on an external inference endpoint and may help with residency, network isolation, and custom controls. It also creates responsibilities that managed services hide: GPU scheduling, model loading, memory pressure, batching, autoscaling, patching, access control, and performance regression testing.

“Open source” is not a complete compliance or cost description. Check the model license, weight provenance, acceptable-use terms, training-data statements, security posture, and redistribution conditions. A model can be openly available while still requiring careful legal and operational review.

Build a requirements matrix before comparing products

Start with requirements, not a shortlist of models. Record the requirement, its priority, the evidence needed to verify it, and the consequence of failure. A requirements matrix makes hidden assumptions visible and prevents a demo from deciding an architectural boundary.

Example requirements matrix for an AIOps LLM
Requirement Priority Verification evidence Failure consequence
Telemetry remains in an approved region Must Architecture and contract documentation Deployment is rejected
Prompts are excluded from provider training Must Current provider terms and configuration Data-processing review fails
Incident summary completes within the SLO Should Representative replay under expected load Operator workflow becomes slower
Model can be replaced without rewriting workflows Should Endpoint contract and adapter test Vendor lock-in increases
Inference works during external-provider outage Could or Must Documented fallback and outage exercise Reduced incident-assistance capability
Monthly spend is predictable Must Usage model, quota behavior, and budget alert Budget or scaling incident

Use four priority values: Must, Should, Could, and Not required. A model that fails a Must requirement should not remain in the running because it performs well on an easier criterion. Also write down the owner of each requirement. Privacy, platform engineering, security, procurement, and incident management may all need to approve different parts of the decision.

Compare privacy and control at the data boundary

The first technical question is not “which model sees the alert?” It is “what exact data is allowed to cross which boundary?” An alert may contain hostnames, usernames, IP addresses, query text, stack traces, customer identifiers, tokens, or fragments of production data. Classify those fields before sending them to any model.

A practical request path looks like this: 

telemetry
  -> field classification
  -> secret and personal-data redaction
  -> prompt construction
  -> policy gateway
  -> model endpoint
  -> output validation
  -> operator or automation

The policy gateway should be explicit about allowed destinations, request size, authentication, timeout, retry, logging, and response handling. Do not log raw prompts by default. Log a request identifier, policy decision, model identifier, latency, token counts if available, and a redacted failure reason.

Vendor-managed deployments require careful review of provider-side retention, region selection, subcontractors, support access, and model-improvement use. BYO deployments move some of this review to the endpoint provider and your gateway. Self-hosted deployments reduce external processing but do not remove privacy risk: prompts can still leak through logs, traces, crash dumps, or operator access.

Control also includes behavior. Ask whether you can pin a model version, set deterministic parameters where appropriate, constrain output formats, disable tool use, and add a local fallback. A deployment with private networking but no version or output controls may still be difficult to govern.

Compare cost without inventing a number

Exact cost depends on provider terms, traffic, context size, hardware, utilization, redundancy, storage, support, and engineering time. A useful comparison therefore starts with a cost model rather than a price claim.

For a managed or BYO hosted endpoint, estimate: 

monthly_cost =
  input_usage_cost
  + output_usage_cost
  + endpoint_or_gateway_cost
  + network_cost
  + observability_cost
  + support_or_commitment_cost

For self-hosting, include the same request-related costs plus the capacity required to keep the service available: 

monthly_cost =
  compute_reserved_or_consumed
  + storage
  + network
  + observability
  + redundancy
  + operations_engineering
  + model_evaluation_and_upgrade_work
  + request_variable_cost

Do not compare a provider’s request estimate with raw GPU rental alone. Include idle capacity, failover capacity, deployment automation, patching, on-call work, and the cost of a slower incident response when the model is unavailable. Conversely, do not assume self-hosting is more expensive at every scale: sustained, predictable usage can change the balance, but that conclusion must come from your measured workload and capacity plan.

Build three traffic scenarios: quiet, expected peak, and incident surge. For each, record requests per minute, input context size, output size, concurrency, timeout, retry policy, and availability target. If you cannot provide these inputs, label the estimate as a planning assumption rather than a forecast.

Reproducible evaluation procedure

The following procedure is designed to compare ownership models without requiring a particular provider or model. It is a method, not a report of completed tests. Run it with sanitized, representative inputs in an authorized environment.

  1. Define the task set. Select a small corpus of incident summaries, alert clusters, runbook questions, and structured extraction tasks. Remove secrets and personal data. Keep the same inputs for every candidate.
  2. Define acceptance criteria. For each task, specify factual accuracy, required fields, prohibited actions, maximum latency, and whether a human must approve the output.
  3. Record the boundary. Document where prompts, outputs, logs, model artifacts, and telemetry are processed and retained.
  4. Run an availability test. Exercise timeout, rate-limit, invalid-response, and provider-unavailable paths. Confirm that the AIOps workflow degrades safely.
  5. Run a privacy test. Send canary values that are safe and authorized for testing. Confirm that redaction occurs before the endpoint and that raw values do not appear in application logs.
  6. Run a change test. Pin the model or image, change one version, and compare output schemas, latency, and acceptance results.
  7. Calculate total cost. Use measured request volumes and documented infrastructure assumptions. Keep usage-based and fixed costs separate.
  8. Score the matrix. Mark each requirement as Pass, Fail, or Unknown. Unknown is not a pass; it identifies evidence still needed.

A minimal endpoint contract can make the comparison repeatable: 

POST /v1/chat/completions
Authorization: Bearer <short-lived-token>
Content-Type: application/json

{
  "model": "approved-model-id",
  "temperature": 0,
  "messages": [
    {
      "role": "system",
      "content": "Return JSON matching the approved incident schema."
    },
    {
      "role": "user",
      "content": "<sanitized-incident-context>"
    }
  ]
}

The placeholder token and incident content are intentional. Never place production credentials or unredacted telemetry in a tutorial, shell history, test fixture, or source repository.

Verification checklist

Before approving an ownership model, verify each claim against current evidence. A useful review packet contains:

  • the data classification and approved processing boundary;
  • the endpoint contract, authentication flow, and network diagram;
  • retention, training-use, region, and support-access statements;
  • model version pinning and rollback procedure;
  • redaction, output validation, and audit-log behavior;
  • quiet, expected-peak, and incident-surge capacity assumptions;
  • failure tests for timeout, quota, invalid output, and endpoint outage;
  • human-approval rules for actions that can change production state;
  • an owner and review date for each requirement.

Verification should be performed in the same integration path that production will use. A vendor dashboard screenshot, a local model demo, or a successful single request is not enough evidence for retention, resilience, or cost.

Failure cases to design for

The provider changes the model

A model update can alter output style, tool behavior, latency, or schema compliance. Mitigate this with version identifiers where supported, contract tests, representative replay, and a rollback or alternate route.

The self-hosted service runs out of memory

Context length, concurrency, batching, quantization, and model size all affect memory pressure. Define a bounded failure mode: reject oversized requests, queue safely, route to a smaller approved model, or return a human-readable fallback. Do not allow an inference retry loop to amplify an incident.

Redaction removes the context the model needs

Aggressive masking can make an incident summary vague or incorrect. Test redaction against task acceptance criteria. Prefer stable placeholders such as <HOST_01> when relationships matter, and document which fields are transformed.

The model produces a plausible but unsafe action

A confident explanation is not authorization to change production. Separate read-only investigation from state-changing tools, validate structured arguments, enforce allowlists, and require explicit human approval for consequential actions.

BYO creates an accidental single point of failure

An endpoint gateway can centralize policy and also become a dependency. Monitor its queue, rate limits, certificates, and upstream routes. Decide whether a degraded mode can summarize locally, use a second approved endpoint, or continue with conventional runbooks.

Decision guide

Ownership model fit by dominant requirement
Dominant requirement Likely fit Reason to challenge the choice
Fastest implementation with limited platform capacity Vendor-managed Data boundary or provider change controls are insufficient
Freedom to change model providers BYO The integration contract is too narrow or endpoint operations are unmanaged
Strict network isolation and infrastructure control Self-hosted The team cannot provide reliable serving, patching, and capacity operations
Predictable, sustained high utilization BYO or self-hosted Actual utilization, redundancy, and engineering costs are unknown
Multiple models for different AIOps tasks BYO gateway Routing and evaluation add complexity that the team cannot support

If all three models pass the Must requirements, prefer the one with the smallest operational surface for the first production release. Keep the endpoint contract portable so that a later change in privacy, cost, or availability requirements does not force a full workflow rewrite.

A hybrid design is often practical: use a managed endpoint for low-risk summaries, route restricted telemetry to a private endpoint, and retain a deterministic non-LLM fallback for critical incident procedures. This is not automatically simpler. It adds routing, policy, evaluation, and observability work, so include those costs in the matrix.

Limitations of this comparison

This article does not identify a specific provider, model, license, region, price, compliance status, or service-level commitment. Those details change and must be verified against the terms and architecture available to your organization. It also does not establish that one ownership model produces better incident outcomes. Model quality depends on task design, context selection, retrieval, tools, prompts, evaluation data, and human workflow.

The framework is intentionally evidence-bounded. Where a claim depends on your environment, the article gives a measurement or review step instead of inventing a result. Your final decision should preserve the raw test inputs, acceptance criteria, assumptions, and evidence dates so it can be revisited when traffic, policy, or model behavior changes.

Conclusion

The question “who owns the LLM?” is really a question about who owns risk, change, and operational work. Vendor-managed models can shorten the path to value. BYO can preserve model choice and policy control. Self-hosted open-source models can provide the strongest infrastructure boundary, but only when the organization is prepared to operate the service.

Build the requirements matrix, classify the data, define the failure modes, and verify every Must requirement with evidence. Choose the model ownership boundary your team can defend during an incident—not just the model that looks best in a demonstration.
